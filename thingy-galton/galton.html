A mechanical proof of the binomial. A ball meeting a peg goes right with probability p, and the row it lands in counts how many rights it took. That is the definition of a binomial random variable, built out of physical Bernoulli trials.
  • Follow one ball. Set the fall speed to slow (or press “Drop 1 ball”) and a single ball falls on its own, trailing its route. The letters under the board are its left/right sequence, and the bin it ends in is nothing more than the number of R’s.
  • Watch the exact pmf appear. The green steps are C(n,k)pk(1−p)n−k, drawn before any ball falls. The blue bars climb to meet them — that is the law of large numbers acting on each bin’s relative frequency.
  • The dashed orange curve is de Moivre–Laplace, the central limit theorem’s first historical special case: Binomial(n, p) ≈ N(np, np(1−p)). Set rows to 4 and the fit is poor; at 20 rows it is excellent.
  • Skew the pegs. With p = 0.1 the pile bunches against the left wall and the normal approximation overhangs into impossible negative bins — the usual guidance is that np and n(1−p) should both exceed about 5.
  • Total variation distance between the measured histogram and the true pmf is plotted below; it shrinks like 1/√(balls), independently of how good the normal approximation is. Two different convergences, happening at once.
